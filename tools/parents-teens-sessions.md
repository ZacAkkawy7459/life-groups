# Parents of Teens & Young Adults — 12-Session Bank

**For:** Claude Code
**Owner:** Zac
**Stream:** `parents-teens`
**Audience:** Parents of children roughly 12–25. Salvation Army outreach.

Build as a stream inside the existing Life Group site. App behaviour, design, NLT handling, highlighting, PDF export and the leader/participant gate are specified in `marriage-life-group-sessions.md`.

---

## Differences from the marriage stream

**Run at 75 minutes**, same as the marriage stream.

**This group needs a stronger confidentiality frame than any other stream.** These parents are talking about identifiable young people who have their own reputations in a small church community, and who did not consent to being discussed. Put this on the opening gate for this stream, above the standard privacy line:

> What's said here about our children stays here. They're not in the room to give their side.

Have the leader say it out loud at the start of every session, not just session one.

**More sessions carry leader notes than in any other stream** — sessions 4, 7, 8 and 11. Several touch mental health, estrangement and family breakdown. The notes are not optional reading.

**Set the countdown default to 22 minutes.**

## The 12 sessions

### 1 — From control to influence

**Premise:** The job changed and nobody sent a memo.
**Scripture:** Luke 2:41–52; Proverbs 22:6

**Beats**
1. Mary and Joseph lose Jesus for three days and find him arguing theology in the temple. Even that family had a version of this.
2. The shift is from control, which you had, to influence, which has to be earned and can be spent.
3. Every attempt to use control after it's gone spends influence. That's the whole mechanism, and most conflict in these years is a parent who hasn't noticed the switch.
4. **Landing:** are you still trying to run a system you no longer administer?

**Questions**
1. Where am I still using control, and how's that working?
2. What have I got influence over, and am I spending it wisely?
3. What did my parents do at this stage that I've copied without deciding to?
4. What would it look like to lead this young person rather than manage them?

**Share:** one place you're going to stop pulling a lever that no longer works.
**Commitment:** one thing you'll stop controlling this month.
**Closing:** You're not losing authority. You're trading it for something that lasts longer, if you'll let it.
**Day 14:** Did you keep your hands off it?

---

### 2 — Listening

**Premise:** Teenagers talk to people who don't react.
**Scripture:** James 1:19; Proverbs 18:13

**Beats**
1. Quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to become angry. That verse is a parenting manual for these years in eleven words.
2. Proverbs says answering before listening is folly and shame. Most of us do it several times a day.
3. Young people test with a small disclosure to see what happens. Your reaction to the small thing decides whether you ever hear the big one.
4. **Landing:** are you a safe person to tell something to?

**Questions**
1. What do I do with my face when I hear something I don't like?
2. When did my child last tell me something difficult, and what did I do with it?
3. What do I interrupt, correct or fix when I should just listen?
4. What would they say if asked whether I'm easy to talk to?

**Share:** one listening habit you're changing.
**Commitment:** one conversation this month where you ask and don't advise.
**Closing:** You want them to come to you at twenty-five. That's decided by what you do at fifteen.
**Day 14:** Did you manage a conversation where you didn't fix anything?

---

### 3 — A faith they choose

**Premise:** You cannot inherit conviction.
**Scripture:** Joshua 24:15; John 4:39–42

**Beats**
1. The Samaritans first believe because of the woman's testimony, then say they believe because they've heard for themselves. Second-hand faith becoming first-hand — that's the whole arc.
2. Joshua's declaration is *as for me and my household* — a leader's stance, not a guarantee about anybody's interior life.
3. A young person going through the motions is not the same as a young person who believes. Pressure produces the first and can prevent the second.
4. **Landing:** are you after their compliance or their conviction? They're not the same, and one costs the other.

**Questions**
1. Am I more concerned with what my child does or what they actually believe?
2. Where have I applied pressure that might be buying compliance at the cost of conviction?
3. What have I let them see of my own faith — including the difficult parts?
4. What would it mean to trust God with this rather than manage it?

**Share:** one place you're going to loosen your grip.
**Commitment:** one honest thing you'll let them see about your own faith.
**Closing:** They have to meet God themselves. Your job is to stay out of the doorway.
**Day 14:** Did you let them see the honest thing?

---

### 4 — Doubt and distance

**Premise:** The father in the story runs. He doesn't lecture.
**Scripture:** Luke 15:11–24; Mark 9:24

**Beats**
1. A man in Mark says *I believe — help my unbelief*, and Jesus doesn't correct him. That sentence is welcome in scripture.
2. In the parable the father gives the son the freedom to go, and the story turns on the father's posture while he's away — watching, waiting, running.
3. He doesn't chase, argue, or withhold. He also doesn't stop looking down the road.
4. **Landing:** what would it look like to hold the door open without standing in it?

**Questions**
1. What's my child questioning, and have I actually asked what they think rather than telling them?
2. What do I fear most here, and is that fear driving how I respond?
3. Where have I made it harder for them to come back?
4. What does waiting well look like for me this year?

**Share:** one thing you're going to stop doing.
**Commitment:** one act of open-door — a message, an invitation, a subject dropped.
**Closing:** The story is not over. You're in the middle of it, which always feels like the end.
**Day 14:** Has the door stayed open?

> **Leader note:** some parents here have a child who has genuinely walked away, and this session will hurt. Don't offer reassurance you can't back — no "they always come back". Let people say hard things without being fixed. If someone is carrying it heavily, follow up privately in the week.

### 5 — Screens and the world they live in

**Premise:** They are not living in a version of your adolescence with better phones.
**Scripture:** Romans 12:2; Philippians 4:8

**Beats**
1. Paul's instruction is about being transformed by a renewed mind, not about withdrawing from the culture.
2. Most parents pick one of two failed strategies: total surveillance, or giving up. Both end the conversation.
3. Your child's online life is a real life with real relationships and real harm in it. Treating it as fake makes you useless to them in it.
4. **Landing:** do you actually know what their world is like, or just what you fear about it?

**Questions**
1. What do I actually know about how my child spends time online?
2. Am I curious about it, or only anxious about it? Can they tell?
3. What's my own relationship with my phone modelling?
4. What's one boundary that's genuinely worth holding, and one I should let go?

**Share:** one boundary you're keeping, one you're dropping.
**Commitment:** one conversation where you ask about their world with genuine curiosity.
**Closing:** You can be a person they talk to about it, or a person they hide it from. Not both.
**Day 14:** Did the conversation happen, and what surprised you?

---

### 6 — Conflict without leverage

**Premise:** Arguing with someone you can't ground anymore.
**Scripture:** Ephesians 6:4; Colossians 3:21

**Beats**
1. Paul twice warns parents against exasperating or embittering their children. The warning exists because it's easy.
2. As leverage disappears, some parents escalate to get it back. That converts a disagreement into a rupture.
3. Not every hill is worth it. Most families are fighting about four things and only one of them matters.
4. **Landing:** which of your current fights would you still pick if you knew it cost you the relationship for a year?

**Questions**
1. What do we actually fight about, and which of those matters in ten years?
2. What do I do when I'm losing an argument with my child?
3. Where have I embittered them, and have I ever acknowledged it?
4. What's one fight I could simply stop having?

**Share:** one fight you're dropping.
**Commitment:** name it, and tell them you're dropping it.
**Closing:** Winning an argument with a seventeen-year-old is the most expensive victory available to you.
**Day 14:** Has the fight stayed dropped?

---

### 7 — When they're struggling

**Premise:** Knowing the difference between a hard season and something that needs help.
**Scripture:** Psalm 34:18; Galatians 6:2

**Beats**
1. The psalm says God is close to the brokenhearted. Not that he prevents the breaking.
2. Ordinary adolescent difficulty and something that needs professional help can look similar from the outside. Duration, function and withdrawal are the things to watch.
3. Parents often delay because seeking help feels like an admission of failure. It isn't, and the delay costs.
4. **Landing:** if you're wondering whether to get help, that wondering is itself information.

**Questions**
1. What have I noticed that I've been explaining away?
2. What stops me from raising it — with them, or with a professional?
3. How am I coping, and who's supporting me?
4. What's one step I could take this month?

**Share:** as much or as little as you want. Nothing here is required.
**Commitment:** one step — a GP appointment, a conversation, a phone call.
**Closing:** You don't have to work out what it is. You have to notice, and then get someone who does.
**Day 14:** Did you take the step?

> **Leader note — read before running this session.** You are not a clinician and this group is not treatment. Your job is to normalise getting help and then get out of the way. Have the details of your local GP, the school counsellor and headspace ready before the session so nobody has to go looking. If a parent describes anything that worries you about a young person's safety, stay with them after the call and help them make contact with a professional that week. Do not carry it alone, and do not let it sit until next month.

### 8 — Your mistakes

**Premise:** Apologising to your child costs you nothing you can't afford.
**Scripture:** James 5:16; Matthew 5:23–24

**Beats**
1. Confess your sins to one another, says James — with no exemption for the senior person in the room.
2. Many of us were raised by parents who never apologised, and absorbed the idea that it undermines authority. It does the opposite.
3. A child who receives a real apology learns that adults can be wrong and survive it. That's a lesson they'll use on their own children.
4. **Landing:** what do you owe your child an apology for, and what's stopping you?

**Questions**
1. What have I got wrong that I've never acknowledged to them?
2. What was I like as a parent in a season I'm not proud of?
3. What do I tell myself to avoid apologising?
4. What would a real apology sound like — no justification attached?

**Share:** as much as you want to.
**Commitment:** one apology, made properly, this month.
**Closing:** It's not too late. It's later than it was, which is the only argument for doing it this week.
**Day 14:** Did you make the apology? How did they take it?

> **Leader note:** go first if you're leading. This session doesn't work if the leader stays clean.

---

### 9 — Letting go

**Premise:** Hannah hands Samuel over, and the text does not pretend it was easy.
**Scripture:** 1 Samuel 1:27–28; Genesis 12:1

**Beats**
1. Hannah wanted a child desperately, got one, and gave him back. Scripture records the act and spares us the sentimentality.
2. Letting go isn't one moment. It's a hundred small releases — the licence, the trip, the flat, the decision you'd have made differently.
3. Some of what you feel is grief, and naming it as grief helps. This is a real loss inside a good thing.
4. **Landing:** what are you holding that it's time to release?

**Questions**
1. What am I still holding that they're ready to carry?
2. What am I afraid will happen if I let go?
3. Who am I when I'm not needed the way I used to be?
4. What's one release I could make this month?

**Share:** one thing you're releasing.
**Commitment:** hand over one decision, fully, without commentary.
**Closing:** You raised them to leave. It working is not the same as it not hurting.
**Day 14:** Did you hand it over — and did you manage not to comment?

### 10 — The two of you, again

**Premise:** The household is quietening. What's underneath?
**Scripture:** Song of Songs 2:10–13; Ecclesiastes 4:9–12

**Beats**
1. For fifteen years the children have been the shared project. When the project winds down, some couples discover they've stopped being a couple.
2. This is a genuine turning point — some marriages rediscover something here and some quietly end. It's worth being deliberate about which.
3. The Song's line is an invitation to come away. That's a decision made by two people who could equally have stayed on the couch.
4. **Landing:** if the children left tomorrow, what would be left between you?

**Questions**
1. What have we stopped doing together over these years?
2. What do we talk about that isn't the children?
3. What am I looking forward to, and does it include the person next to me?
4. What's one thing we could start now rather than when they leave?

**Share:** one thing you're starting.
**Commitment:** one thing booked, dated, in the calendar.
**Closing:** Don't wait for the house to empty to find out. Start now, while there's time to fix what you find.
**Day 14:** Is it still in the calendar?

> **Leader note:** single parents and those whose marriages have ended will be here. Frame this as "the closest adult relationship in your life" and mean it — for some that will be a friend or a sibling, and that's a full answer, not a lesser one.

### 11 — The long silence

**Premise:** The father in the parable spends most of the story waiting.
**Scripture:** Luke 15:20; Psalm 13:1–2

**Beats**
1. Psalm 13 opens with *how long* — asked four times, of God, with no immediate answer. That prayer is in the Bible for people in this exact position.
2. The parable's father sees his son *while he was still a long way off*, which means he was looking. The waiting was active.
3. Estrangement and distance carry a particular shame because everyone assumes a reason. Most people carry it silently for years.
4. **Landing:** you are allowed to say this out loud. Tonight is the room for it.

**Questions**
1. Is there distance in my family that I've never named?
2. What do I tell people, and what's actually true?
3. What have I tried, and what have I not tried?
4. What would it mean to wait well rather than wait bitterly?

**Share:** only what you want to. Silence is a full answer tonight.
**Commitment:** one act of waiting well — a message sent with no expectation, or simply choosing not to chase.
**Closing:** You are eleven months into a group of people who now know something true about you. Let them carry it with you.
**Day 14:** How are you? One line is enough.

> **Leader note — the heaviest session in this stream.** Say at the start that nobody has to share and mean it. Don't fill silences. Don't offer reassurance about outcomes you can't promise. Don't let the group move to problem-solving — if someone starts giving advice, gently redirect. Check in privately during the week with anyone who spoke, and with anyone who didn't.

### 12 — Blessing

**Premise:** Adult children still need to hear it from you.
**Scripture:** Numbers 6:24–26; Genesis 27:27–29

**Beats**
1. The blessing in Numbers is spoken over people — a parent's voice saying something true about a person's identity and future.
2. Genesis takes blessing so seriously that a family tears itself apart over one. It carried weight; it still does.
3. Most adults can tell you exactly what their parents said about them, good or bad, decades later. You are still writing that.
4. **Landing:** does your child know what you actually think of them? Not your worries. What you admire.

**Questions**
1. What do I genuinely admire about this young person?
2. What have I said more often — my concerns, or my admiration?
3. What would I want them to carry from me for the rest of their life?
4. What's stopping me saying it plainly?

**Share:** one thing you admire about your child.
**Commitment:** say it to them. Out loud or written, this month, in words they can keep.
**Closing:** Twelve months ago you were trying to hold on. Export the year and read what you wrote in session one. Then go and say the thing.
**Day 14:** Did you say it? What happened?
